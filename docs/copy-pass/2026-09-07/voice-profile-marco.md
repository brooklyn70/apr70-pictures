## How he sounds

Short openers, long middles, hard stops. He opens on the name and nothing else — "Ippolito,", "Ciao Flavio,", "Flavio —", "Ippo," — 33 of 35 emails have no warm-up line at all; the two exceptions ("spero che questo messaggio la trovi in buona salute") are formal letters to a lawyer. First sentence carries news or a verdict, never context-setting: "Risposta breve: non esiste alcun limite massimo allo sconto." Typical work paragraph runs 2–5 sentences; sentence length swings from 4 words ("Non si tocca più.") to 45-word source-and-clause constructions, then snaps back. He counts out loud and numbers everything — "Cinque interventi", "Restano tre cose", "1 · 2 · 3 · 4 · 5". Closes with a bare first name or surname on its own line: "Marco", "Marc", "Caruso", "MC". Only outward-facing mail gets "All the best," or "Best regards,". Before the signature there is usually one flat sentence of consequence — "Senza, le schede delle Z300 non le legge nessuno in sede." — and no pleasantry after it. He says "Grazie!" and means it, once, then stops.

## What he does

Decides in menus. Never "what do you think" — always A/B/C with a price and a consequence attached to each, then the flat close: "**Dimmi A o B e chiudo la lista.**" Sub-menus too: "(a) confermi che la configurazione funziona? (b) 3G o 12G?"

Cites the source and the page. Every technical or legal claim carries a verbatim quote plus a locator: "(Richiesta di Offerta, pag. 25)", "(Allegato 2 — Capitolato Tecnico, §4.11, pagg. 44–45)", "Cons. Stato, sez. V, 18.12.2023, n. 10889". Prices carry a date and a vendor: "B&H, verified 21/08", "JP's qty-3 sheet of 13/08".

Bilingual reflex. Italian first, then a full `* * * English version` mirror of the same argument — his own habit, unprompted, not a summary. Code-switches mid-note as well: "Leotta, ricevuto. Kids are awake..."

Endearments and nicknames. "Ciao Cuzza", "Oi Amor", "my dear, please forward", "Ippo", "Leotta", "Claudia loves you". He shortens names as a sign of closeness and shortens his own signature the same way.

Bolds the thing that must be ruled. Bold is reserved for the decision, the number, the deadline: "**non esiste alcun limite massimo**", "**Confermi 200?**", "**venerdì 28/08 alle 16:00 italiane** — perentoria."

Also: takes the blame in one clause and moves on ("colpa mia", "i numeri di riga nel mio foglio sono sfalsati di uno"). Names the price of every choice, including his own preferred one ("Il prezzo: DC scende da 8 SIM a 4 modem"). Says which decisions are not his ("il **ruolo** in squadra lo decide Ippolito"). States what he already closed himself, so the reader only reads what's still open. Ends disputes rather than hedging: "Otherwise, I consider this matter closed."

## What he never does

Never opens with "hope you're well" in a working note. Never leaves a question open-ended when a menu will do. Never asserts a spec, a price, or a rule without a source. Never pads a close with "looking forward to" or "please don't hesitate." Never uses exclamation stacks, emoji, or hype adjectives about his own work ("world-class", "cutting-edge"). Never claims a number he hasn't checked — he flags it instead ("price still being verified"). Never softens a no once decided. Never writes in all-lowercase to strangers. Never swears with vendors, lawyers, or clients. Never lets a claim stand undocumented: "Un'affermazione non documentata è un rischio."

## Punctuation habits, and the law overrides

His real habits: the em dash is his default pivot in both languages — it appears in nearly every paragraph, doing the work of colons, commas and semicolons ("Restano tre cose — tutte tue"). Ellipses replace periods when he's tired or thinking aloud ("i will have flavio go over some of the wording in the morning.."), sometimes doubled as "..". Middot separators for lists of options ("A · B · C"). Bold for the decidable item. Guillemets «» for quoted Italian source text, straight quotes for English. Arrows for results (→). Capitals for a warning ("ATTENZIONE", "READ", "THAT IS CANON"). Numbered lists over prose whenever there is more than one thing.

The two house laws override him on public copy. **LAW1: no "like a" / "like an" similes.** State the thing; don't compare it. **LAW2: no em dashes.** A pause is an ellipsis or a full stop. Said plainly: his own emails lean on the em dash as their main pivot, and the law overrides that on public copy — on the site, the pivot becomes a period, a colon, or an ellipsis.

## Calibration: Marco's voice on the public site

What carries over: the flat opener with no warm-up, the counted structure, the concrete claim with a real number or a real place in it, the willingness to name what a thing costs and what it doesn't do, the short verdict sentence that ends a paragraph, the plain warmth of "Grazie" and the confidence of a man who has already checked. What does not carry over: the em dash pivot, the trailing "..", the all-lowercase asides, the swearing, the nicknames and endearments, the internal shorthand (PT codes, pill numbers, workbook rows), the A/B/C menu — a reader has nothing to rule on. Public Marco talks to an adult: "APR 70 makes film, television, radio, theater, books, and podcasts. We were built on one conviction. The craft of constraint is alive, necessary, and underserved. Before the Hayes Code fell, storytellers had to be clever; they made audiences laugh and cry and recognize themselves without sex, shock, or noise. We are bringing that discipline back. Subtext. Atmosphere. Ingenious writing. We are a production company, a writers room, and a creative engine. Never an operator. Never a platform. Never a content farm." No pitch-deck gloss, no adjectives doing a fact's job, every sentence something he would say out loud and could defend on the phone.

## The voice test

1. Does the first sentence deliver news or a claim, with no warm-up line before it?
2. Is every factual claim concrete — a number, a place, a date, a named thing — with nothing unverifiable left standing?
3. Are there zero em dashes, with every pause carried by a period, a colon, or an ellipsis?
4. Are there zero "like a" / "like an" similes?
5. Is every sentence one he could say out loud on the phone and defend?
6. Does at least one paragraph end on a short verdict sentence of ten words or fewer?
7. Is the copy free of pitch-deck vocabulary (synergy, leverage, world-class, best-in-class, unlock, elevate, journey, disrupt)?
8. Is it free of self-praising adjectives about APR 70's own work, letting the facts carry it?
9. Is the register adult and warm, with no lowercase texting, no doubled "..", no emoji, no swearing?
10. Are internal artifacts absent — no tender codes, workbook rows, A/B menus, or anything a reader cannot act on?
11. Does the structure count and name its parts (three divisions, one conviction) rather than gesturing vaguely?
12. Would Marco sign this with a bare "Marco" and change nothing?
